Plan vs Design

The second lifecycle verb produces a technical design document — architecture, decisions, a risk register — yet it's called plan. The word names the artifact after the wrong thing, and collides with the verb that does the actual planning.

The legacy-file question (§7) was settled in favour of rename all + rewrite cross-links. This brief is kept as the decision record; it did not become a tracked /spectastic.spec after all.

The verb after spec produces stack picks, an architecture sketch, ADR-shaped decision cards, a grounding ledger, and a risk register — a design document. It's named plan, a word that means schedule of work, which is exactly what the next verb (tasks) delivers. So the name misdescribes the artifact and overlaps its own sibling. The field is split on the fix: Kiro calls this stage design.md; spec-kit calls it /plan — and their contents are the same (tech stack + architecture). The recommendation: rename plan → design, full scope. There are no external users, so the churn is a one-time cost and the name finally matches what the tool actually does. The slogan is "design the how, plan the work."

1 · The tension

spectastic's lifecycle is principles → spec → plan → tasks → implement → propose → apply → triage. The second verb answers how we'll build what the spec describes. Look at what it actually emits: a stack selection, a required inline-SVG architecture sketch, ADR-shaped <spec-decision> cards with stable D-NNN ids, a grounding ledger of verified-vs-assumed facts, and a risk register. That is a technical design document by every ordinary meaning of the term.

A plan is a schedule of work. A design is a decision about shape. This verb decides shape.

Two problems follow from the name. First, it misdescribes the artifact: "plan" reads as project management — timelines, sequencing, who-does-what — and drags that baggage into a document that contains none of it. Second, and worse, it collides with its own downstream sibling. The verb that produces the actual schedule of work — the ordered, parallelizable, test-first breakdown — is tasks. That is the plan. Naming the design stage "plan" leaves the real plan stage without the word, and asks a reader to hold two overlapping meanings one step apart in the same pipeline.

This isn't a cosmetic quibble. The verb's own procedure already speaks the language of design — it walks a Principles check, scaffolds <spec-decision> cards on the ADR shape (Status / Context / Decision / Consequences), and gates on a "must-tier decision grounded" affirmation. The content has been "design" all along; only the label says "plan."

2 · What the field calls this stage

The two leading spec-driven tools produce nearly identical middle-stage content and name it in exactly opposite ways. That disagreement is the whole decision in miniature.

Kiro names it design

Kiro (AWS) splits a feature into three files: requirements.md, design.md, tasks.md. The middle file is a "technical design document including architecture decisions, sequence diagrams, and technology stack choices" (Kiro · Specs). Note what sits inside Kiro's design: the technology-stack choice. The thing one might argue is "planning, not design" — picking the stack — Kiro files squarely under design.

spec-kit names it plan

GitHub's spec-kit runs Spec → Plan → Tasks → Implement. Its /plan command is where you "provide your tech stack and architecture choices … addressing technical implementation details" (github/spec-kit). Same payload as Kiro's design file — stack plus architecture — under the opposite label.

The content's ancestry is design, not plan

spectastic's decision cards are ADRs — the Architecture Decision Record shape (Context / Decision / Consequences), a documented, widely-adopted pattern (adr.github.io). An ADR's home is the design document; a project schedule doesn't carry ADRs. The lineage of what this verb emits — ADRs, design docs, RFCs — is uniformly "design." spectastic's <spec-decision> element is, quite literally, an ADR container.

So the field gives no clean precedent to copy — it gives a coin already flipped twice, landing on both sides. The tie-breaker isn't popularity; it's which word describes the artifact honestly, and which one avoids a collision spectastic uniquely has (a separate tasks verb spec-kit and Kiro also have, but whose overlap with "plan" neither seems to have felt).

3 · The case for design

D-1 · Rename the second verb plan → design

Status
Recommended
Context
The verb emits a stack pick, an architecture sketch, ADR decision cards, a grounding ledger, and risks — a technical design document. Kiro names this exact content design.md (Kiro · Specs); the ADR lineage the cards descend from (adr.github.io) lives in design docs. The current name describes none of that.
Decision
/spectastic.plan becomes /spectastic.design; the artifact plan.html becomes design.html. The verb keeps its whole procedure — Principles check, grounding gate, ADR cards — only the name changes to match the content.
Consequences
+ The name finally describes the artifact. + Removes the semantic overlap with tasks (the real work plan). + Aligns with the ADR/design-doc lineage and with Kiro. − A one-time rename across the verb surface, the template, the core/CLI modules, and 64 existing plan.html artifacts (see §5). − A short divergence from spec-kit's vocabulary for anyone who knows it.

D-2 · "Design the how, plan the work" — the two verbs stop overlapping

Status
Recommended
Context
spectastic uniquely carries a separate tasks verb whose output is the plan of work — ordered, parallelizable, test-first. With the design stage also called "plan," the pipeline holds two "plans" one step apart.
Decision
Draw the line by meaning: design decides shape (stack, architecture, decisions); tasks plans the work (the ordered breakdown). Each verb owns one honest word. tasks keeps its name — it is the real plan, and renaming it would only move the collision.
Consequences
+ Each stage's name is unambiguous and mutually exclusive. + Reads cleanly aloud: "design the how, then plan the work." − Relies on the reader internalising that "the plan" now lives under tasks; the lifecycle prose must make that explicit once.

4 · The case for keeping plan (steel-man)

The honest counter-argument, taken at its strongest:

D-3 · Keep plan — familiarity, breadth, and churn

Status
Not recommended
Context
Three reasons pull toward the status quo. (1) Familiarity — spec-kit's Spec → Plan → Tasks → Implement is the vocabulary a growing cohort already knows (github/spec-kit). (2) Breadth — the verb also produces a grounding ledger and a risk register, which aren't strictly "design"; "plan" is a bigger umbrella that arguably covers them. (3) Churn — a rename touches the template, both command modules, the model policy, the lifecycle prose, and 64 existing artifacts.
Decision
Rejected. The breadth argument is the weakest link and it breaks on the evidence: Kiro folds the stack choice itself into design.md, so "design" already stretches to cover the toolchain — and grounding and risk are the rationale for design decisions, which is what an ADR's Context and Consequences hold. "Design" is not too narrow. Familiarity is real but shallow (a one-line alias could preserve it if ever needed), and the churn is a one-time cost with no external users to break.
Consequences
+ Steel-manned honestly; the breadth concern is answered, not waved away. − Diverging from spec-kit's word is a genuine, if small, cost paid once.

The 64 existing plan.html files are the real weight. Renaming them re-keys every historical cross-link and every verify.html that traces into a plan. Whether to migrate them (with redirects) or freeze them as legacy is a decision the follow-on spec must make deliberately — it is not settled here.

6 · Recommendation

Rename plan → design, full scope. The artifact is a design document; the name should say so; and doing it now — while the only user is the author — costs nothing but one focused sweep. The evidence is not that "design" is more popular (the field is split), but that it is more honest: it names the content, and it clears the collision with tasks that spectastic uniquely has.

Outcome: rather than become a tracked /spectastic.spec, the rename was executed directly (no external users to justify the ceremony). The migration decision (§7's open question) was settled in favour of rename all + rewrite cross-links; the sweep landed 30 Jul 2026.

7 · Questions raised — and how they resolved

Every question below was open at recommendation time and settled when the rename landed.

  1. Legacy-artifact migration. Are the 64 existing plan.html renamed to design.html, or left as legacy? Resolved: rename all 64 via git mv (history preserved) and rewrite every cross-link and #D-NNN anchor — no redirects. The corpus is fully consistent.
  2. Breadth of "design." The verb also emits grounding and risk; does the prose need to say "design" covers them? Resolved: yes — the command doc frames the artifact as the design doc, and a CLAUDE.md note records that grounding and risk are the design's rationale (ADR Context/Consequences), not a separate thing.
  3. Backward-compat alias. Keep a one-release plan→design alias? Resolved: clean break, no alias — there are no external users. (A "plan the implementation" trigger phrase is kept on the verb, since that's still how people speak.)
  4. Hard-coded strings. Does anything hard-code the literal plan.html? Resolved: swept every generator, provider, and link builder; verified by a full-corpus validate at 0 errors and the pre-commit gate — no dangling plan.html reference remains.

8 · Out of scope (for this /doc)

  • Performing the rename was out of scope for this brief — but it has since been done directly (landed 30 Jul 2026), rather than via a tracked /spectastic.spec.
  • Splitting the stage into two verbs (design + a separate plan) — verb-count discipline stands; the eight-verb core is a deliberate constraint.
  • Renaming any other lifecycle verb — only the design/plan collision is in question; tasks, spec, and the rest keep their names.
