Considerations · EU AI Act evidence

Rationale, and its basis, kept current

Annex IV asks for the design choices, their rationale, and the sources they rest on — written before market and kept up to date. That is not a report you generate. It is a trail you leave.

Status Draft Type Compliance-mapping survey Author Brian Corbin · @briancorbinxyz Created 24 Jul 2026 Sources 5 primary, verified 24 Jul 2026 Companion Knowledge bases for spec authoring Read time

For a team building a high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act, the burden that arrives in August 2026 includes technical documentation (Article 11 / Annex IV) that records how the system was developed, which design choices were made and why, what sources and third-party tools were used, and how it changed over its lifecycle — drawn up before the system reaches the market and kept current. A spectastic trail — spec → plan → <spec-decision> with a cited source → <spec-changelog> — is contemporaneous evidence toward two of Annex IV's nine sections (§2 development & design rationale, §6 lifecycle changes), and the corpus proposal in the companion doc is what lets a decision cite its basis, not just its reasoning. This is a real and specific win. It is also narrowly bounded: it is documentation evidence, not the Act's runtime event-logging (Article 12), not a conformity assessment, and spectastic is an authoring tool, not itself a high-risk system. The honesty about the boundary is what makes the claim usable.

1 · What the Act actually asks for

The EU AI Act obliges the provider of a high-risk AI system to prepare and keep current a body of technical documentation (Article 11), whose contents are enumerated in Annex IV — nine sections spanning the system's lifecycle. Two of them are strikingly close to what a spec-driven trail already produces.

Annex IV §2 — development process & design
Calls for "the methods and steps performed for the development", the third-party tools used, the design specifications and "key design choices including the rationale", the data used, and dated test records. This is the heart of it: not just what was built, but why each choice was made.
Annex IV §6 — lifecycle changes
Calls for "relevant changes made by the provider … through its lifecycle" — the record of how the system evolved.

Two constraints on that documentation matter as much as its contents. It must be drawn up before the system is placed on the market, and it must be kept up to date (Article 11). Documentation with those two properties cannot be credibly reconstructed the week before an audit — it has to be written as the work happens. That timing requirement is the whole reason a trail beats a report.

2 · Why a spec trail fits — the design-rationale shape

A spectastic project already leaves exactly this trail, contemporaneously, because the lifecycle forces it:

Annex IV wants…The trail already carries…
Key design choices including the rationale (§2)<spec-decision> — Context / Decision / Consequences, the ADR shape, one per choice
The basis for a choice, sources used (§2)grounding="verified" citing a corpus source ID — the design fact and the document it rests on
Methods, steps, third-party tools (§2)The plan's technical-context and the Assisted-by trailer recording the model that helped
Data used (§2)Requirements + the cited corpus documents, each with provenance frontmatter
Relevant changes across the lifecycle (§6)<spec-changelog> + the propose → apply → archive record, each change dated and reasoned
The differentiator is the basis, not the reasoning.

Plenty of tools can capture a decision and its reasoning. What Annex IV §2 asks for that most development artifacts lack is the basis — the source a rationale rests on. A rationale that says "we chose T+1 because that is the settlement cycle" is an assertion; one that says "we chose T+1 per KB-001@2024-05-28, a committed copy of the governing rule" is evidence. That is precisely the move the companion doc's corpus proposal makes possible: it turns a decision's grounding from assumed into verified against a document an assessor can open. The EU AI Act is, in effect, an external requirement for the exact discipline spectastic's grounding grammar already expresses — which is why the corpus is not a nice-to-have for a regulated team but the thing that makes the trail admissible.

3 · The boundary — what this is not

The claim is only useful if its edges are honest. Four of them, stated plainly.

This is not Article 12 logging. Article 12 requires a high-risk system to automatically record events (logs) over its lifetime — inputs, outputs, decision points at run time — for traceability of the system in operation. That is a property of the deployed software, generated at inference, and spectastic has nothing to do with it. A spec trail is development-time documentation (Article 11 / Annex IV); the two are explicitly separate in the Act and must not be conflated.
  • It is evidence, not a conformity assessment. Annex IV is one input to a conformity process that also involves a risk-management system (Article 9), quality management, and — for many systems — a notified body. A spec trail contributes to the documentation; it does not certify anything.
  • Spectastic is not a high-risk AI system. It is an authoring tool. The obligations fall on the provider of the system being built; spectastic can help that provider produce §2/§6 evidence, and is itself out of scope.
  • Coverage is partial by design. Of Annex IV's nine sections, the trail speaks strongly to two (§2, §6), moderately to a couple more (§4 metrics justification, §7 standards decisions), and not at all to several (§8 declaration of conformity, §9 post-market monitoring). Claiming the whole of Annex IV would be the overclaim that discredits the real part.
  • A trail is not automatically admissible. Evidence still has to be organised to an assessor's expected shape. The gap between "we have the decisions" and "here is the Annex IV §2 pack" is real work — see the recommendation.

4 · Recommendation — an evidence map, at the enterprise tier

Do not build a compliance module. Build the one thing that turns an existing trail into an assessor-shaped artifact: a mapping.

  1. An Annex IV evidence map. A derived view — in the spirit of the existing generated verify.html — that aggregates, by reference, which requirements, decisions, cited sources and changelog entries speak to which Annex IV section. It asserts nothing new; it arranges what the trail already holds into the shape an assessor reads, and is honest about the sections it cannot fill.
  2. Gated to the enterprise profile. This is enterprise-tier concern by construction — most projects are not building high-risk AI systems, and the machinery should be invisible to them. It rides the profile ladder the same way supply-chain provenance and formal accessibility already do.
  3. Grounding is the precondition, not an add-on. The map is only worth generating if decisions actually cite their basis, which is the corpus proposal in the companion doc. Without the corpus, the map documents reasoning; with it, the map documents evidence. Sequence accordingly: corpus first, map second.
  4. Claim the part, never the whole. The generated map must state, on its face, that it is contributory evidence toward §2 and §6 — not a conformity assessment and not complete Annex IV coverage. The honesty is a feature: an assessor trusts a tool that names its own limits.

The upside, stated without inflation: a team building a high-risk system on spectastic gets a running head start on the most labour-intensive, least-automatable part of Annex IV — the design-rationale-and-basis record — as a by-product of the lifecycle it was already following, written contemporaneously as Article 11 requires rather than reconstructed under audit pressure.

5 · Ceilings and open risks

  • The Act is young and its harmonised standards are still settling. The precise documentary shape assessors will expect for §2/§6 is not fully fixed; an evidence map should track the standard, not hard-code one reading of it.
  • A convincing-looking evidence pack can mislead. A well-formatted map over thin or wrongly-grounded decisions is worse than none — it lends false assurance. The corpus's silent-conversion-loss risk (companion doc) propagates straight into compliance evidence.
  • "Contributory evidence" is a claim a lawyer must bless. This survey is an engineering read of Annex IV, not legal advice; whether a given trail satisfies a given assessor is a question for counsel, and the artifact should say so.
  • Scope creep toward "compliance tool" is the trap. The moment this tries to assess conformity rather than arrange evidence, it takes on liability it should not hold. The mapping stays descriptive.
