Considerations · Backlog audit

What is actually left to build

Two hundred deferred slugs accumulated across nine months of surveys and specs. Most are not backlog. This is the register that says which are.

Status Draft Type Derived register & recommendation Author Brian Corbin · @briancorbinxyz Created 31 Jul 2026 Method Repository scan · classified · spot-checked Read time

Draining the two contract surveys raised a fair question — should the others be drained too? The answer needed a number, and the number turned out to be hard to get: the repository marks a landed slug two different ways, so a strict scan and a loose scan disagreed by 37. This register settles it. Of 205 slugs planted, 49 have landed, 4 are recorded ceilings that were deliberately declined, 12 need a human call, and 140 are genuinely pending. The split that matters is not the total but the origin: only 37 came from a considerations survey, where slugs are planted as suggested next artifacts and map cleanly onto specs. The other 103 sit in specs' own out-of-scope blocks, where they are boundary markers — "not this slice" — and were mostly never meant to become anything. Recommendation: drain the survey backlog, selectively; leave the spec-side markers where they are; and fix the annotation convention that made this uncountable.

1 · What this is, and how far to trust it

A derived register of every TBD- slug planted anywhere in this repository, classified by whether the work has landed, was deliberately declined, or is genuinely queued. It exists because the backlog had become uncountable: two different conventions mark a landed slug — a struck ~~slug~~ and a prose (landed …) annotation — so neither a strict nor a loose scan gives the right answer on its own, and two attempts at counting it produced 192 and 155 before this one.

Classification uses four signals, in order: a spec directory whose name matches the slug topic exactly; a spec changelog that names the slug as resolved; a struck annotation in the project notes; a prose landed-annotation. A fifth, weaker signal — a spec whose name is a token-subset of the slug — is not treated as resolution but surfaced separately in §5, because most of those are follow-on work rather than the same work.

The counts are good, not exact. Spot-checked at 8/9 and 11/12 against hand-verified cases before the near-miss pass was added. The residual error is concentrated in slugs whose name drifted from the spec that resolved them, and those are listed rather than silently bucketed. Treat a single row as a prompt to look, not as a verdict.

6 · Recommendation

Three moves, smallest first.

  1. Fix the annotation convention. The reason this audit was needed is that a landed slug is marked two ways. Pick one — the struck ~~slug~~ form is the more scannable and already the more common — and normalise the rest. This is the cheapest of the three and the only one that stops the problem recurring.
  2. Drain the survey backlog selectively, largest cohort first. The deployment-operations survey alone accounts for 9 of the 37, and its slugs group as naturally as the contract ones did — enforce categories, profile axes, and design-stage prompts. The corpus-extraction set is the other coherent cohort. Neither needs to happen in one pass.
  3. Leave the spec-side markers alone. They are doing their job where they are: recording a boundary a slice chose not to cross. Promoting them wholesale would invert the meaning of an out-of-scope block.
Do not treat backlog size as a defect to be driven to zero. A deferred slug is often the most honest artifact in the repository — the recorded ceilings especially, where a survey looked at something and said no. Draining them would convert considered refusals into committed work, which is the opposite of what the deferral was for. The measure worth watching is whether the survey backlog grows faster than it drains, not whether the total ever reaches zero.
