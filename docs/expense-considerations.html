Considerations · Lifecycle economics

Expense

What will it cost — in agent time, human attention, and tokens — to build this? The honest answer is "it depends, and your intuition is wrong." So expense estimates the way the evidence says you must: in ranges, conditioned on the mode, and only as confidently as the data earns.

Status Draft Type Design brief (a /doc — research + considerations, feeds a future /spectastic.spec) Author Brian Corbin · @briancorbinxyz Created 25 Jun 2026 Sources Primary + foundational, fetched + quoted 25 Jun 2026 (§8) Read time

spectastic already estimates one expense — a spec's read time — and sizes a spec's scope with <spec-budget>. It cannot yet answer the question every adopter asks: what will it cost to build this? expense is the proposed ex- verb (explain · explore · expense) that estimates the build-expense of a spec, slice, or run across three dimensions — agent wall-clock, human attention, and tokens/$. The design is shaped by an uncomfortable evidence base: software estimation is chronically unreliable, large models are poorly calibrated about their own cost, and with AI even experienced developers' intuition is measurably backwards (METR: they expected a 24% speedup, were 19% slower, and still believed it helped). So expense refuses the confident point estimate. It borrows the methods that admit uncertainty — relative sizing against a reference class of recorded actuals, probabilistic ranges not points, and a spike to tighten the estimate only when the value of the information beats the spike's own cost. Crucially the estimate is a function of the run mode (--effort × decider × hands-off), which is the part nobody else models. It ships as a verb that computes + records actuals and a <spec-expense> gauge that displays the range — and it answers the open "Cost ceiling" question in the workflows brief.

1 · The opportunity — spectastic estimates one expense already

Every spectastic artifact carries a data-reading-time estimate — the wall-clock cost of a human consuming it — and a <spec-budget> gauge that sizes its scope (words, requirements, read-time) and flags an amber band at 70%. So the project already estimates a read-expense and already renders a live cost gauge. What it cannot yet answer is the build-expense: given this spec, what will it cost — in agent time, human attention, and tokens — to take it through design → tasks → implement?

That question is now load-bearing. The workflows brief proposes hands-off pipelines and value-ranked slicing whose every dial (effort, decider, fan-out) moves cost — and it leaves an explicit open question: "what is the per-run budget cap, and does the pipeline degrade gracefully as it approaches the ceiling?" expense is the brief that answers it. But the answer has to begin by admitting how hard the question is.

2 · What the field does

A grounded read, quoted from primary sources (§8). The theme is consistent and inconvenient: this is one of the least reliable numbers in software, and AI has made it less intuitive, not more.

2.1 · Estimation is unreliable — and AI makes intuition wrong

Start with the finding that should humble any estimate. In a randomized controlled trial of 16 experienced open-source developers across 246 real tasks, METR found that "when developers are allowed to use AI tools, they take 19% longer to complete issues — a significant slowdown." The striking part is the perception gap: the same developers "expected AI to speed them up by 24%," and even after living through the slowdown "still believed AI had sped them up by 20%." A 40-point gap between felt and actual productivity — from the people doing the work.

This sits on top of a deep base rate. The planning fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) is "the tendency of people to underestimate the completion times and costs for difficult tasks" (outside view), and it is robust even when you know about it. The cone of uncertainty adds the cruel timing: estimates are needed earliest, when they are least accurate. The lesson expense takes: a confident point estimate is not honesty, it's theatre.

2.2 · Token cost — meterable after, weakly predictable before

Tokens look like the easy dimension — they're counted exactly, after the fact. Before the fact they are not. A Stanford Digital Economy Lab study, How Do AI Agents Spend Your Money?, found that "frontier models fail to accurately predict their own token usage (with weak-to-moderate correlations, up to 0.39)" — and that input tokens, not output, are the primary cost driver in agentic tasks (context re-reading, tool results) and the harder one to foresee. The control lever the platforms ship is the effort parameter — "control how many tokens Claude uses… trading off thoroughness for token efficiency" — which is exactly why expense must condition on it (§3.2). The failure mode without a budget is a loop that re-queries and re-reads until a "simple" task has quietly spent a fortune.

2.3 · LLMs at effort estimation — moderate, and not magically comparative

Can the model just estimate its own effort? Moderately. Work on LLM story-point estimation reports that zero-shot, "LLMs can predict story points better than supervised deep learning models trained on 80% of the data" — encouraging, but the absolute correlations are weak. And one tempting shortcut doesn't hold: "it is not easier for LLMs to predict comparative judgments than to directly estimate the story points," though comparisons "serve as few-shot examples to improve" accuracy. Worth stating plainly, because the relative-sizing rationale below is a fact about humans, not a free win for models — for an agent, comparison is useful as grounding examples, not as an inherently easier task.

2.4 · The methods that transfer all admit uncertainty

Relative sizing
People estimate by comparison, not arithmetic: "a team doesn't have to precisely estimate how long a given product backlog item will take. Instead, the team only has to match it to other similarly sized items" (Mountain Goat). Comparison is lower-variance than absolute guessing.
Reference-class forecasting (the outside view)
Forecast from a class of similar past cases, not bottom-up from this one's details — the "outside view… looks at whether there are similar situations — a reference class — that can provide a statistical basis." It is the documented antidote to the planning fallacy.
Probabilistic ranges, not points
The #NoEstimates / forecasting tradition: "providing a range of outcomes along with the likelihood of those outcomes will lead to better decision-making than providing a single value" (Keogh). Monte-Carlo over recorded throughput turns history into a distribution.
Spikes governed by the value of information
When uncertainty is too wide, buy information with a time-boxed spike — but only when it's worth it. Decision theory is precise here: the Expected Value of Perfect Information is "the price one would be willing to pay to gain access to perfect information," and "if one is offered knowledge for a price larger than EVPI, it would be better to refuse the offer." That is the rule for your "ironic" spike (§3.4).

3 · The design — expense for spectastic

3.1 · Three dimensions, each a range

expense reports three numbers, and not one of them is a point:

  • Agent wall-clock — how long the autonomous stretches run.
  • Human attention — time a person spends at checkpoints, reviews, and decisions. The dimension everyone omits, the hardest to estimate, and itself a range scaled by the reviewer's proficiency: an expert reads a plan at lower cognitive load and decides by pattern-recognition where a novice deliberates. spectastic already has the axis — explain's wheels | completion | independent ladder (§3.2, §3.6).
  • Tokens / $ — metered exactly after the run; estimated as a band before it. Tokens are the stable unit; dollars are a display conversion that drifts with model pricing.

Each renders as an interval with a stated confidence — agent 8–20 min · human 5–15 min · 120k–340k tokens, 80% CI — never a bare "about 30 minutes."

3.2 · The estimate is a function of the mode

This is the part the literature doesn't model and the part you flagged: expense = f(work, mode). The same work costs very differently depending on how the lifecycle is run, and the workflows brief already names the dials. The estimate must be conditioned on them, not averaged over them:

--effort (§9.2)low → max↑ (more thinking)~↑↑ — the dominant multiplier

--effort is the agent-side dial; --proficiency is its human-side counterpart, borrowing explain's ladder (default wheels, the conservative novice assumption — a wider human-time band). So expense doesn't answer "what will this cost" — it answers "what will this cost at --effort=high, decider human at wheels proficiency, checkpointed," and lets you compare modes. A hands-off, all-agent, max run and a gated, novice-reviewed, low run are different economic objects for the same spec.

3.3 · Relative sizing against a reference class of recorded actuals

Because absolute prediction is weak (§2.1–2.3), expense sizes the work by comparison: this slice is "about the size of" past slices, and those past runs have recorded actuals. That is the outside view, made mechanical. The first job of the verb is therefore not to estimate — it is to record: after every real run, capture the actual agent-time, human-time, and tokens for that unit of work, tagged by its mode. Over time the project grows its own reference class, and the estimate becomes "runs like this one, at this mode, landed in this band."

Honest prerequisite. spectastic records none of this today. The AIProvider interface (packages/core/src/types.ts) returns only text — no token counts, no timing. Building the reference class requires extending it to return usage metadata. Until then, expense can only do the weaker a-priori estimate; the strong version is gated on that instrumentation — designed in §3.7.

3.4 · Confidence, and the spike that costs to estimate

An estimate's width should reflect how much it's trusted. expense derives confidence from two things spectastic already has or is building: the grounding ledger (a plan resting on assumed facts deserves a wider band than one that's verified) and the depth of the reference class (three similar past runs beats zero). A low-confidence estimate is a wide estimate — and that is the signal to consider a spike.

Here is your irony, made rigorous. A spike — a time-boxed investigation — can narrow the band, but it spends the very time and tokens you were trying to estimate. So expense doesn't always spike; it applies the EVPI rule (§2.4): run the spike only when the expected value of the information it buys exceeds its own cost. If a tighter estimate wouldn't change any decision — you're going to build it either way — the spike is waste, and refusing it is the right call. spectastic already owns the spike primitive (the grounding ledger's spike status); expense adds the decision rule for when pulling it is economical.

3.5 · The cone narrows down the lifecycle

One estimate is wrong; a re-estimate at each stage is a discipline. The band is widest at spec (scope only), tighter at plan (decisions + grounding known), tighter still at tasks (the work is enumerated). expense re-runs at each artifact and the gauge shows the cone closing — which doubles as a health signal: a band that isn't narrowing as you descend the lifecycle means the work is still genuinely uncertain, and that is worth knowing before implement.

3.6 · Attention is a budget

Human attention isn't just a per-checkpoint cost — it's a finite, depletable reserve, and the cleanest model for it is the one SRE already uses for reliability: the error budget. Google's is exact — "an error budget is 1 minus the SLO of the service," and when a service "has exceeded its error budget… we will halt all changes and releases other than P0 issues or security fixes" (SRE workbook). An attention budget is the human mirror: a session has only so much, and every checkpoint, review, and decision spends it.

The mechanics are well-evidenced. Attention is the scarce resource — "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" (Simon, 1971) — and working memory is "extremely limited in both capacity and duration" (cognitive load theory). Operations already names the failure: alert fatigue arises "when alerting systems exceed the cognitive capacity of the people responsible for responding," because "every alert requires a decision… over the course of a shift, they accumulate" into decision fatigue, so that "the effectiveness of alerting decreases as alert volume increases" (SIGNL4). A pipeline that gates a tired human at every step is an alert storm by another name.

So expense estimates the attention draw of a run — roughly checkpoints × decision-type × (1 / proficiency) — and the actionable direction mirrors the token degrade-gracefully guardrail, on the human side: as a session's budget runs low, spend fewer gates and escalate the decider down (human → agent → panel). Decision fatigue is met the way alert fatigue is — by making fewer, higher-value demands.

What this is not, and what it can't yet do. The budget is per-session, not project-level — and its starting reserve is unknown and variable: someone arriving at the end of a long day may begin already depleted, and nothing here can measure that. Acting on a low budget also demands near-invisible ergonomics; a clumsy "you seem tired, shall an agent decide?" is worse than no prompt at all. So this slice commits only to the framing and the estimable part (the draw, from checkpoint count × proficiency); seeding/measuring the reserve and the ergonomics of acting on it are surveyed in §3.8 — catalogued, deliberately not committed.

 The budget framing rests on cognitive load and alert/decision fatigue — both operationally evidenced — and pointedly not on "ego depletion," whose finite-willpower claim failed large-scale replication. The reserve is real and bounded; the discredited mechanism is avoided.

3.7 · Measuring actuals — the usage record

§3.3 named the prerequisite: without recorded actuals the estimate stays the weak a-priori kind. The good news is that this half is cheap and mechanical — tokens are counted exactly, and spectastic is already throwing the count away. The Claude provider (packages/core/src/providers/claude.ts) receives the Anthropic response — which carries a usage object (input_tokens, output_tokens, cache_creation_input_tokens, cache_read_input_tokens) — and returns only the text, discarding it. Capturing it, plus a wall-clock measurement (the bench/ performance.now() pattern), yields a per-call usage record.

The shape should follow an existing standard rather than invent one. The OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions already name these fields — gen_ai.usage.input_tokens ("the number of tokens used in the GenAI input (prompt)"), gen_ai.usage.output_tokens, gen_ai.usage.cache_read.input_tokens ("served from a provider-managed cache"), and gen_ai.request.model — and every observability tool (Langfuse, OpenLLMetry) captures exactly this per trace:

UsageRecord = {
  model,             // gen_ai.request.model
  input_tokens,      // gen_ai.usage.input_tokens
  output_tokens,     // …usage.output_tokens
  cache_read_input_tokens?,
  cache_creation_input_tokens?,
  wall_clock_ms,     // span duration
  cost_usd?          // derived at display, not stored
}

Sum the records across a run, tag by mode (§3.2), and you have one reference-class entry — "a 022-sized slice at --effort=high cost this." The brief stops at the record shape; the future spec fixes the exact AIProvider signature (today's chat() returns a bare string — it would return text + usage). Cost in dollars is derived at display from a per-model price table, never stored (pricing drifts, §5).

3.8 · The attention-budget mechanism — what the research says, and why we don't commit

§3.6 deferred the hard half: how to seed a per-session attention reserve and how to act on it. The research is real and points somewhere — but this is where the doc deliberately surveys rather than decides, because the subject of measurement is a person, not a token.

You can only measure attention subjectively, and imperfectly
The standard instrument, NASA-TLX, is a "subjective workload assessment tool" — a "weighted average of ratings on six subscales," self-reported, and explicitly "not a measure of the true biological workload." Biometric alternatives are more objective and more invasive. There is no cheap, accurate, non-creepy way to read a reserve.
Timing matters more than frequency
Interruptions cost far more mid-task than at a task boundary (Bailey et al.), and attention residue means a poorly-timed gate taxes the next task too. So when a checkpoint fires is as important as how many.
The ergonomic moves are "change defaults," not "prompt"
Progressive disclosure — "show users only a few of the most important options… a larger set upon request" — and the lean last responsible moment (batch a decision until deferring it would cost an option) both reduce load without asking the human anything.
The SRE burn-rate is the right signal shape
"Burn rate is how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget" (SRE workbook). An "attention burn-rate" — decisions-per-hour against the session reserve — is a better trigger than a binary "budget spent."

A recommended direction falls out of this, if a future spec builds it: opt-in only; act by changing defaults and batching, never by prompting; time gates to task boundaries; trigger on a burn-rate, not a threshold. But the brief stops there on purpose. Seeding the reserve means either nagging the user (adds load) or inferring their state (privacy, creepiness, and an unreliable guess from §3.8's own evidence). Whether spectastic should infer a person's fatigue at all is a values decision, and it belongs to a spec with its owner in the room — not to a research brief.

The asymmetry between §3.7 and §3.8 is deliberate. Measuring tokens is mechanical, exact, and standards-backed, so it's designed. Measuring a person is none of those, so it's only surveyed. The honest move is to let the two halves differ.

4 · Form & where it lives

Two surfaces, mirroring the <spec-budget> precedent (a gauge backed by logic):

The verb — /spectastic.expense <spec-id> [--effort … --decider …]
An ex--family extended verb (explain · explore · expense). It computes the mode-conditioned ranges, and — after a real run — records the actuals back into the reference class. Opt-in, like its siblings; it sits beside the lifecycle, not inside it.
The gauge — <spec-expense>
A header component, sibling to <spec-budget>: where budget sizes scope, expense estimates build cost. It renders the latest range for the artifact's current mode, with its confidence band, and degrades (per P-4) to plain text with JS off.

The verb is the engine; the gauge is the display. Both are gated on the usage instrumentation (§3.3), so the honest first slice is the a-priori estimator plus the actuals recorder — the reference class can only grow once runs are being measured.

5 · Guardrails & honest limits

Never sell a point as certain
Every figure is a range with a confidence. METR is the standing reminder that confident estimates here — even from the practitioners — are routinely, invisibly wrong.
The human owns the commitment
expense informs; it does not promise. The planning fallacy is robust even when known, so a number from an agent is an input to a human's decision, never a contract.
Record actuals or stay weak
Without the measured reference class, every estimate is the unreliable a-priori kind. The instrumentation isn't a nice-to-have; it's the thing that makes the estimate improve.
Bounded budgets that degrade gracefully
This is the workflows brief's "cost ceiling" answer: a run carries a token/time budget and, as it nears the cap, the pipeline degrades — fewer voters, shallower panels, single-pass deciders — rather than halting mid-task. expense sizes the cap; the workflow honours it.
Tokens are the unit; dollars are a view
Store and reason in tokens (stable); convert to $ only at display, against current pricing, because pricing drifts.
Respect the attention budget
The human-side mirror of degrade-gracefully (§3.6): decision fatigue is met by fewer, higher-value gates — not by gating a depleting reviewer at every step. When a session's budget runs low, escalate the decider down before quality does.

6 · Open questions

  1. Human-attention time — the estimable part and the residual. The draw is estimable: checkpoint count × decision-type, scaled by proficiency (§3.2, §3.6). What stays unknown is the starting reserve (a reviewer may begin a session already depleted, and nothing here measures that), raw availability/interruption, and whether the band can ever be tight enough to commit to. The proficiency tier narrows it; it does not close it.
  2. Should spectastic infer a person's attention at all? §3.8 surveys the mechanism (opt-in self-report + proxies; act by changing defaults; burn-rate trigger) but deliberately stops short of committing — seeding the reserve means either nagging the user or inferring their fatigue, and whether to do the latter is a values call for a spec, not a research brief. The estimable draw ships; the reserve stays a documented choice.
  3. Where do recorded actuals live? A new reference-class ledger artifact, a sidecar per spec, or a project-level store? It must survive across specs to be an outside view, which pulls toward a project-level file — but that is a new persistent surface to design.
  4. The EVPI threshold. "Spike only when the information is worth it" needs an operational rule: how is the value of a tighter estimate quantified when the downstream decision (build / defer / split) is itself fuzzy?
  5. Cross-project reference classes. A shared corpus of actuals would forecast far better than one project's history — but that crosses a privacy and comparability line (every team's "medium slice" differs). In-project only, by default?
  6. Unit drift. If dollars are a display conversion, where does the pricing table live, and how stale is too stale before a shown "$" misleads?

7 · Out of scope (for this /doc)

  • Building /spectastic.expense, the <spec-expense> gauge, or the reference-class store — this brief feeds a future /spectastic.spec.
  • Building the AIProvider usage-capture change — the record shape is designed in §3.7; the interface change itself feeds a future /spectastic.spec.
  • Committing to an attention-budget mechanism — §3.8 catalogues the research + a recommended direction, but any design that infers user state is deliberately left to a future spec with its owner in the room.
  • Promising a precise single-number estimate — the evidence (§2) makes that a non-goal by design, not an omission.
