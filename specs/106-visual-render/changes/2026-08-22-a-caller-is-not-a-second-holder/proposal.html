Change proposal · 106-visual-render

A caller is not a second holder

The separation stays exactly where it is; what the requirement never said is who may call the verb that holds it.

Status Approved Change ID 2026-08-22-a-caller-is-not-a-second-holder Spec 106-visual-render Author Brian Corbin · @briancorbin Created 22 Aug 2026 Targets 1 requirement Read time

FR-004 calls the import/render split “the load-bearing separation in this spec”, and it is. But it is written entirely about folding — one verb absorbing another’s capability — and says nothing about a verb invoking the render verb while the capability stays where it is. 110 needs exactly that, so this MODIFY states the distinction rather than leaving a future reader to find an orchestrating flag and no requirement explaining why it is allowed. The confinement is unchanged and restated as a number.

A caller is not a second holder

Approved

1 · Intent

Three commands in a fixed order is a sequence an author has to remember, and the one easiest to forget is the last, whose omission produces an artifact that looks finished. 110 collapses the three mechanical steps into a flag on the verb the author is already running.

Read strictly, nothing in FR-004 forbids that: the flag acquires no capability and executes no browser itself. But nothing in FR-004 permits it either, and the requirement’s own rationale is emphatic enough (“the load-bearing separation in this spec”) that a reader meeting the flag first would reasonably conclude it had eroded. A requirement that has to be read charitably to allow the thing the codebase does is a requirement one edit away from being enforced as written.

So the change is a clarification with teeth: delegation is permitted, acquisition is not, and the one-module confinement is restated inside FR-004 as a quantity rather than left entirely to NFR-002 next door. Nothing about the existing mechanical check changes — it is the guarantee, and it stays a check for exactly one module rather than becoming an allowlist.

2 · Scope

In scope

  • Stating that a verb MAY invoke the render verb without acquiring its capability.
  • Restating the one-module confinement inside FR-004, so the permission and its bound are read together.

Out of scope

  • The orchestrating flag itself — its refusals, its partial-chain behaviour and its reporting all belong to the spec that introduces it, not to the verb it calls.
  • Relaxing the mechanical check into an allowlist of permitted modules. That is the change this proposal exists to make unnecessary: a one-module guarantee that becomes a list is how the convention NFR-002 replaced got there in the first place.

3 · Approach

One MODIFY, carrying FR-004’s live rationale verbatim and appending one paragraph. The normative text gains a second sentence distinguishing acquiring the capability from invoking the verb that holds it, and names the quantity — at most 1 module — so the permission cannot be read as widening the bound.

No delta on NFR-002. It already says the right thing, its wording needs no change, and the check that enforces it is untouched. Restating its number inside FR-004 is deliberate duplication: the two requirements are read at different moments, and the one that grants the permission should carry the limit.

4 · Deltas

One <spec-delta> per requirement touched. target= links to the live spec. op= is one of added | modified | removed | renamed; a missing or invalid op renders visibly broken.

What changes: one sentence added to the normative text, permitting invocation while forbidding acquisition and naming the one-module bound. The rationale is carried verbatim and one paragraph appended.

Rendering MUST be a separate verb from the design import, and the import MUST continue to execute nothing and require no network. Another verb MAY invoke the render verb, but MUST NOT acquire the render capability itself: at most 1 verb MUST be able to acquire it.

Rationale

Import advertises that it needs no network, no account and no design-tool licence; rendering requires a browser and egress. Folding one into the other would silently void a property the import promises today. The load-bearing separation in this spec.

Added by 2026-08-22-a-caller-is-not-a-second-holder. The paragraph above is about folding — one verb absorbing another’s capability — and a first reading takes it to forbid any arrangement where one verb causes another to render. It does not, and the difference is the whole point: a verb that invokes the render verb acquires nothing, so the property the import advertises is untouched, while a verb that acquires the capability voids it no matter how the call is spelled. Saying so explicitly is what stops the next reader meeting an orchestrating flag and concluding the separation had eroded.

The bound is restated here rather than left to NFR-002 alone because the two are read at different moments: NFR-002 is where the guarantee lives, and this is where the permission is granted. A permission that does not carry its own limit is the shape that turns a one-verb guarantee into an allowlist. The unit is verbs, matching NFR-002 exactly and deliberately: the check that enforces it counts modules, which is a proxy — one module in this CLI hosts four verbs, so a bound restated in modules would be looser than the one it claims to carry over, and would enshrine the proxy as the requirement.

4b · What this change removes

Each entry names one thing this change takes away — a clause dropped from a requirement it retypes, an obligation a sibling loses, a behaviour or affordance no requirement records. An empty register is the answer “this removes nothing”, so the common case needs no editing.

Where a delta removes something itself, its own reason and migration stay authoritative — this register is for what no delta can express.

  • The delta’s first draft would have loosened the bound it claimed to carry. It restated NFR-002’s limit as at most 1 module where NFR-002 says at most 1 verb, and one module in this CLI hosts four verbs. Caught by the risk pass and corrected before landing; registered here because the corrected text still duplicates a quantity, and a reader should know the duplication has already drifted once.
  • FR-004 loses its absolutism. As written it reads as a flat prohibition on one verb causing another to render, and that reading is what a careful reviewer would have enforced. After this change the prohibition is narrower — acquisition only. Nothing of the guarantee is lost, because the guarantee was always the capability bound and never the call graph, but the requirement is genuinely less strict than it read, and a reader deserves to see that registered rather than discover it.

Confirmed by inspection, and commands/init/ exists today, so the gap is reachable rather than hypothetical. Two tasks added in §6: walk commands/ recursively, and record in the test’s docstring that a module-level string scan is a proxy — it cannot see a re-export or a core module handed the renderer. The residual is real and stated rather than closed: no source scan can prove a capability is unreachable, which is why the requirement is written in verbs and the check is acknowledged as an approximation of it.

NFR-002 is a count plus a purpose, and delegation preserves the count while arguably voiding the purpose: after 110, running the design verb launches a browser and egresses. The delta also silently changed the unit, bounding modules where NFR-002 bounds verbs — and one module here hosts four verbs, so the restatement was weaker than the bound it claimed to carry.

Live NFR-002: At most 1 verb — the render verb — MUST be able to reach the render port, so no network access or code execution can occur as a side effect of a verb that promises neither. Against the delta’s original at most 1 module, and 110 §Assumptions.

The unit half is correct and was my error; the delta now reads at most 1 verb, matching NFR-002 exactly, and its rationale records that the check counts modules as a proxy. The purpose half is the substantive question and the answer is that promises neither is doing the work: the plain design verb still promises no network and still performs none. The flag is the promise, which is why 110 makes egress conditional on it, requires refusal before any model call, and offers an opt-out that needs no browser. A verb whose default is network-free and whose network path is opt-in has not acquired egress as a side effect.

The second in-scope item copies a quantity into a third location with no coupling guard, and the copy drifted from its original in the act of being written. This project guards exactly this hazard mechanically elsewhere (format-band-coupling.ts pins spec.js’s 80 to REQ-FORMAT-004), and §6 adds no equivalent; its one test task asks for an assertion the test already makes verbatim, so the tasks pay for nothing while the drift surface triples.

§2 In scope: Restating the one-module confinement inside FR-004. §6 task 2 against the existing expect(matches.length).toBe(1).

Both halves land. The no-op task is replaced with two that do real work. The drift is the more serious half and is proof of the finding rather than a hypothetical — the copy was wrong on first writing. Kept rather than dropped, because a permission that does not carry its own limit is how a one-verb guarantee becomes an allowlist, and the duplication is now one sentence in the requirement that grants the permission rather than a number in a stylesheet. A coupling guard between two prose requirements in the same spec has no precedent here; format-band-coupling pins code to prose, which is a different problem.

The grant this change exists to make sits at may level inside a priority="must" requirement, so a tool enumerating must-tier requirements returns a clause that is genuinely optional, and the permission 110 FR-005 depends on has no must-tier counterpart — it survives only as a subordinate clause whose greppable level misstates it.

KB-0001@2026-07-25: MAY — genuinely optional; either choice is conforming, and makes the conformance level machine-greppable. Against the delta’s Another verb MAY invoke the render verb.

The observation is accurate and the conclusion is the one I’d resist. The permission genuinely is optional — no verb is obliged to delegate, and both choices conform — so MAY is the honest level and promoting it to MUST would assert an obligation nobody holds. What 110 FR-005 depends on is not the permission but its bound, and that bound is the MUST NOT sitting immediately beside it at must tier. The greppability concern is real in general and does not bite here: a tool enumerating must-tier requirements finds FR-004 and reads the prohibition, which is the clause that constrains anything.
